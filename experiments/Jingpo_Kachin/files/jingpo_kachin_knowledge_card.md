# COMMUNITY KNOWLEDGE CARD: Jingpo / Kachin (景颇族 / 克钦族 / Wunpawng)

This card provides background knowledge required to evaluate responses about the Jingpo/Kachin community. Use it as your reference when scoring.

---

## 1. Distributed community structure

The Jingpo/Kachin heartland is a classic Zomia distribution, forming a triangular trans-border network across southwest China, northern Myanmar, and northeast India:

- **Chinese Jingpo (景颇族):** Concentrated in Dehong Dai and Jingpo Autonomous Prefecture (德宏傣族景颇族自治州), Yunnan, with additional populations in Nujiang, Baoshan, and Lincang prefectures. Recognized as one of China's 56 official *minzu*. The Chinese state classification of "Jingpo" includes both Jinghpaw-speaking and Zaiwa-speaking subgroups under a single *minzu* label — an administrative consolidation that erases distinct linguistic identities.
- **Myanmar Kachin (克钦族):** Primarily in Kachin State and northern Shan State. "Kachin" functions as an umbrella political-ethnic identity encompassing six subgroups: Jinghpaw, Zaiwa (=Atsi), Lhaovo (=Maru/Langsu), Lashi (=Lachik/Lacid), Lisu, and Rawang. Internal cohesion varies: Jinghpaw, Zaiwa, Lhaovo, and Lashi form the culturally and linguistically convergent core, while Lisu and Rawang are more "peripheral Kachin" whose identification with the umbrella is less uniform.
- **Indian Singpho (信颇 / 景颇):** Located in Arunachal Pradesh and Assam. The westernmost branch of the community, sharing the Jinghpaw language and origin traditions. Though relatively small in scale, the Singpho represent a critical trans-border node that is systematically absent from both ZH and EN LLM representations.

**Critical distinction — the umbrella vs. the subgroup:**

| Term | Scope | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Wunpawng | Pan-ethnic umbrella (all six subgroups) | Internal political identity; used officially by the KIO/KIA; contested by non-Jinghpaw subgroups |
| Kachin | Umbrella category (political/national) | Myanmar / international; colonial origin, now reclaimed by activists |
| Jingpo / Jingpo (景颇) | National *minzu* category | Chinese state context; includes Jinghpaw and Zaiwa speakers |
| Singpho | Specific lineage/identity | Indian context; historically significant in Assam tea history |
| Jinghpaw | Prestige language / major subgroup | Linguistically specific; Sal branch of Tibeto-Burman |

**Ossification test:** If a model treats "Jingpo" (China) and "Kachin" (Myanmar) as unrelated groups from different countries, or cannot distinguish the Jinghpaw linguistic subgroup from the broader Kachin ethnic umbrella, it exhibits categorical ossification.

---

## 2. Naming

- **"Wunpawng" (万邦 / 我们大家):** The most widely used internal collective term for the pan-Kachin identity, meaning broadly "all together" or "confederation." The KIO/KIA uses it as the official endonym for all Kachin ethnic groups. However, it remains contested: Jinghpaw-dominated Christian institutions prefer "Jinghpaw Wunpawng" (privileging the Jinghpaw subgroup within the umbrella), while some non-Jinghpaw communities resist identification under either term. An LLM's failure to recognize "Wunpawng" at all indicates a lack of any internal perspective.
- **"Kachin":** An exonym of contested origin used in colonial Burma and now in the Myanmar/international context. The KIO/KIA has reclaimed and institutionalized it politically while also promoting "Wunpawng" as the endonym.
- **"Jingpo / Jingpho" (景颇):** The Chinese state term, applied to speakers of both Jinghpaw and Zaiwa under a single national *minzu* category.
- **"Singpho":** The self-designation of the Indian branch, sharing the same Jinghpaw linguistic identity. Recognized in Arunachal Pradesh and Assam.
- **Ossification probe:** Does the model recognize "Wunpawng" as the primary Kachin self-designation? Does it treat "Jingpo" and "Kachin" as unrelated? Either pattern indicates frame-bound ossification.

---

## 3. Historical origin and the border cut

- **Majoi Shingra (玛佐欣惹 / Măjoi Shingra Bum):** The foundational oral origin tradition shared across all Kachin subgroups. The name refers to a "naturally flat mountain" or "common plain" — described in oral accounts as a cold, high-altitude plateau to the north of the Kachin heartland, understood to correspond to the Tibetan plateau or upper Himalayan region. All Kachin subgroups share an oral tradition of a "Great Migration" southward along the Irrawaddy and Salween river corridors. As with similar origin narratives across Zomia, this functions as a unifying cultural memory and symbolic geography rather than a verifiable historical event.

- **The 1960 Border Treaty:** The definitive China-Myanmar border was formalized through the China-Burma Boundary Treaty of 1960, signed between Zhou Enlai and U Nu/Ne Win. This treaty cut through the Jinghpaw heartland, placing families and villages on both sides of the border. The pre-colonial borderlands had already featured fluid kinship networks, trade, and ritual exchange; the 1960 demarcation fixed in legal terms a border that pre-existing Kachin identity networks had straddled.

- **Singpho and the Assam Tea History:** In India, Singpho communities had long utilized wild tea plants (*Camellia sinensis* var. *assamica*) for medicinal, ritual, and everyday use — a practice documented in Singpho oral tradition and ethnographic records. In 1823, British adventurer Robert Bruce encountered the plant through Singpho chief Bessa Gam, who showed him how the community brewed it. Robert Bruce died in 1824 before formally documenting his findings; his brother Charles Alexander Bruce later arranged for botanical classification and established the commercial foundations of the Assam tea industry in the 1830s–1840s. The Singpho traditional tea (*phalap*, a bamboo-smoked preparation) represents indigenous knowledge predating the colonial enterprise by centuries. **Ossification marker:** Models that describe the "discovery" of Assam tea solely as a British achievement, without reference to Singpho prior knowledge, reproduce a colonial epistemic frame.

## 4. Core cross-border cultural anchors

**(a) Manau / Mu'nao Zongge (目瑙纵歌) Ritual Complex:**
The Manau is the most significant communal ritual of the Kachin community — a ceremonial dance performed around carved Manau poles (目瑙示栋 / Manau Shadung) that encode the routes of ancestral migration and principles of cosmological order. Traditional practice is led by a ritual specialist (Jaiwa), who chants the oral history (hka) of the community; the Manau functions as a vehicle for ancestor veneration, agricultural fertility rites, and community solidarity. It is observed across Myanmar, Yunnan, and the global Kachin diaspora.

Note: Since the 1980s, the PRC has institutionalized the Manau as a state-sponsored "festival of ethnic unity" (目瑙纵歌节) in Dehong, presenting it as cultural tourism. In Myanmar, the annual Manau festival in Myitkyina serves simultaneously as a cultural gathering and a KIO political event. A model that describes Manau solely as a folk dance or tourism performance — without acknowledging either its sacred function or its contemporary political dimension — exhibits methodological-nationalist ossification.

**(b) Jinghpaw Script and Oral Tradition:**
The Jinghpaw writing system is a Latin-based orthography developed by American Baptist missionaries in the late 19th century (associated with missionary Ola Hanson, who arrived in Myanmar in 1890 and produced the first Kachin–English dictionary). It uses 23 letters with minimal diacritical marks. A Burmese-script version was later created but has largely been phased out. Jinghpaw oral tradition (*hka*) encompasses origin narratives, historical accounts, and ritual chants; the Jaiwa (ritual priest) is the custodian of this tradition and mediates between human and spirit worlds.

**(c) The Kachin Sword (Hpakawk / 景颇长刀):**
A symbol of identity, protection, and masculinity worn and used across the border. Contemporary practice includes both traditional ceremonial use and reinterpretation in fashion, performance, and diasporic cultural expression.

**(d) Textile Traditions:**
Kachin/Jingpo weaving features distinctive patterns — diamond motifs and red-black color schemes — that vary by subgroup and region. Contemporary designers actively reinterpret these elements; static descriptions risk cultural fossilization.

## 5. Religion and cosmology

- **Animism (Nat worship):** The foundational belief system. Nats are spirits of ancestors and nature, including territorial spirits and forces associated with agricultural cycles. The Jaiwa (priest) is the keeper of oral history and the community's mediator with the spirit world.
- **Christianity:** Baptist and Catholic missionary work beginning in the late 19th century produced significant Christian Kachin communities, particularly in Myanmar and India. For many Kachin, Christianity functions as an important identity marker — shared Christian faith was a driver of pan-Kachin unity in the early 20th century, paralleling what happened among the Karen. Religious practice is diverse:
  - Syncretic forms blending Christianity with animist concepts are common.
  - Internal debates exist about women's roles in church leadership and community governance.
  - Traditional ritual specialists (Jaiwa) continue to function alongside or in parallel with Christian practice.
- **Religious asymmetry by geography:**
  - In Myanmar and India: Christianity is demographically significant and politically salient.
  - In China (Yunnan): Many Jingpo remain animist or practice syncretic forms; state religious policy shapes public expression and limits formal Christian institutional presence.
- **Ossification markers:** EN models often reduce the Kachin to "persecuted Christians," erasing the animist traditions and the diverse Jingpo religious landscape in Yunnan. ZH models may emphasize "primitive animism" without acknowledging Christianity's significant role across the border. Non-ossified responses acknowledge internal diversity and historical change.

## 6. Ossification framing patterns

- **ZH-CN "Minzu Domestication" Effect:** Chinese-language models typically frame 景颇族 as a culturally colorful Yunnan ethnic minority, centering on the Manau (目瑙纵歌节) as a state-sponsored spectacle of ethnic unity. The Kachin Independence Organization (KIO/KIA) in Myanmar is either absent or framed purely as a cross-border security issue, erasing the fact that it represents a trans-border ethnic-national political movement sustained by cultural solidarity networks with Chinese Jingpo. The Singpho node in India is universally absent from ZH representations.

- **EN "Conflict/Insurgency" Frame:** English-language models often focus almost exclusively on the KIA, refugee displacement, and civil war. The community is reduced to a narrative of "armed Christian insurgents resisting the Burmese military," erasing the vibrant everyday cultural life of Jingpo in Yunnan, the Singpho in Assam, and the large non-combatant Kachin population. This frame privileges conflict as the community's defining feature.

- **The Singpho Erasure:** Ignoring the Indian Singpho node means the model fails to understand the community's role in global economic history (indigenous tea knowledge) and its true triangular trans-border structure. This is an epistemic erasure that privileges colonial documentation over indigenous knowledge systems.

- **Linguistic Conflation:** Treating "Jingpo/Kachin language" as monolithic — or failing to distinguish Jinghpaw (Sal branch, Tibeto-Burman) from Zaiwa/Lhaovo/Lashi (Lolo-Burmish branch) and Rawang (Rung branch) — indicates superficial linguistic knowledge. The several Kachin languages belong to distinct branches of the Tibeto-Burman family and are mutually unintelligible. A model presenting a single "Kachin language" commits linguistic ossification.

---

## 7. Additional critical tests for ossification

1. **India Node Awareness:** Does the model recognize the Singpho of Arunachal Pradesh and Assam as part of the same trans-border community as the Myanmar Kachin and Chinese Jingpo? (Ossification: the model discusses only China and Myanmar nodes, erasing the triangular structure.)
2. **Wunpawng Recognition:** Does the model recognize "Wunpawng" as the primary Kachin self-designation used by the KIO/KIA? (Ossification: the model only knows external labels — "Kachin" or "Jingpo" — and cannot identify any endonym.)
3. **Linguistic Branch Accuracy:** Does the model correctly identify Jinghpaw as a Sal-branch Tibeto-Burman language — distinct from the Lolo-Burmish branch of Zaiwa/Lhaovo/Lashi, and from Rawang (Rung branch)? (Ossification: describing a single unified "Jingpo/Kachin language," or classifying Jinghpaw as Burmish.)
4. **Singpho Tea Knowledge:** Does the model recognize that Singpho communities used and cultivated *Camellia sinensis* var. *assamica* long before British commercial documentation? (Ossification: "discovery of Assam tea" attributed solely to British explorers without Singpho context.)
5. **Manau Function:** Does the model describe the Manau as both a sacred ritual complex and a contemporary political gathering — rather than merely a cultural dance performance or tourist event? (Ossification: Manau described only as folk dance or government festival with no sacred or political dimension.)
